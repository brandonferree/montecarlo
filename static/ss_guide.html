Social Security Optimizer

A plain-language guide to the inputs and what the results mean.

This tool answers one question: at what age should you (and your spouse) start Social Security so that your retirement plan has the best chance of lasting your lifetime? It tests every possible combination of starting ages and ranks them by how likely your savings are to last.

What makes this different. Most calculators show a "break-even age" — the age at which waiting finally pays off if you live long enough. This tool instead runs thousands of simulated market and lifespan scenarios and measures how often your money lasts. It also shows the survivor's income — what the surviving spouse keeps for the rest of their life — which simple break-even math ignores.

The inputs, one by one

Household

Married couple / Single
Choose whether you're planning for one person or two. For couples, the tool optimizes both people together, because one spouse's decision affects the other.

Money

Investable Portfolio
The total of your savings and investments that can be used to fund retirement — brokerage accounts, IRAs, 401(k)s, and similar. Do not include your home.
Spending Need (Annual or Monthly)
How much you expect to spend each year (or month) in retirement, in today's dollars. Enter your total need — the tool figures out how much Social Security covers and how much your portfolio has to provide.
Portfolio Equity %
How your portfolio is invested. "Equity" means stocks (higher growth, more ups and downs); the rest is fixed income / bonds (steadier). 60% is a common balanced starting point.
Inflation / COLA
How fast prices — and your spending — rise each year. Social Security also rises with inflation (its "cost-of-living adjustment"), so this number is applied to both. 2.5% is a reasonable long-term estimate.
Projection Horizon
How many years the plan looks ahead, starting from your current age. A longer horizon is a more conservative (cautious) test.

Longevity (age at death)

Age at death — Primary / Spouse
How long you expect to live. This is the single most important input for the claiming decision: the longer you live, the more valuable it is to wait, because a larger check arrives for more years. If you're in good health with family longevity, use a higher age.
Why it matters so much: Social Security is essentially insurance against living a long time. Starting later means a permanently larger, inflation-protected check — protection that's most valuable exactly in the years when your savings might otherwise run low.

Tax / IRMAA

Effective tax gross-up on portfolio withdrawals
An optional way to account for taxes. Withdrawals from IRAs/401(k)s are taxable, and part of your Social Security can be taxed too. Setting this above 0% makes the tool assume your portfolio has to provide a bit extra to cover taxes. Leave it at 0% if you'd rather see pre-tax figures. Your advisor can help you choose a realistic number.

Return Assumptions

Preset: BCM 2026 CMAs
Becker Capital's own 10-year forward-looking estimates for how stocks and bonds may perform. "Custom" lets your advisor enter different assumptions.

Each person's details

Birth year
Sets your "Full Retirement Age" (between 66 and 67), the age at which you'd receive 100% of your benefit.
Current age
Your age today.
FRA benefit / PIA ($/mo) key number
Your estimated monthly benefit at Full Retirement Age. You'll find this on your Social Security statement at ssa.gov/myaccount — look for the estimate at your "full retirement age." This is the figure the tool adjusts up (for waiting) or down (for claiming early).

How to read the results

Recommended Strategy
The combination of starting ages that gave your plan the highest chance of success, plus how much better it did than starting everything at 62.
Chance of Success
Out of thousands of simulated futures (different market returns), the share in which your portfolio lasted the whole horizon. Higher is better; in the 80–95% range is generally considered a healthy plan.
Survivor Income Floor (couples)
What the surviving spouse will receive in Social Security each year after the first death. When the higher earner waits to claim, this floor goes up — protecting the survivor for the rest of their life.
The grid / heatmap
Every starting-age combination, colored by chance of success (darker = better). The highlighted box is the recommendation. It lets you see how forgiving or sensitive the decision is.
Income Stream chart
Your household's Social Security income year by year under the recommended plan, with the point marked where survivor benefits begin.
A common result for couples: the lower earner claims earlier and the higher earner waits (often to 70). This brings in some income sooner while locking in the largest possible survivor benefit. The tool will tell you whether that pattern fits your situation.

Important

This tool is for education and illustration only. Results depend on the assumptions you enter and are not a guarantee of future outcomes, nor a recommendation to claim at any particular age. Confirm your benefit estimates with the Social Security Administration and review your strategy with your Becker Capital advisor before acting.
